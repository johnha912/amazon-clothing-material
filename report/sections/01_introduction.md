# Introduction

Material decides how a garment feels, how long it lasts, and whether the buyer is satisfied, yet Amazon rarely stores it as a searchable field. It sits in free-text feature bullets as a composition string such as "90% Polyester, 10% Spandex", so a shopper who wants only cotton shirts, or a buyer with a wool allergy, has no reliable filter. Recovering this field automatically would make a large catalog searchable by a property shoppers care about, and it would tell sellers and marketplaces whether that property is worth stating clearly.

We study three linked questions on the Clothing, Shoes and Jewelry category of Amazon Reviews 2023 (Hou et al.), about 7.2 million products. Task A asks whether a product's dominant material can be recovered from its listing text; its target is the fiber with the highest percentage in the composition string, and the composition strings themselves are removed from the input so that the task is genuine inference rather than lookup. Task B asks whether material helps predict the average star rating (1 to 5) of products with at least 5 ratings, together with price and department. Task C, added after instructor feedback on the proposal, starts from the observation that a star average is a noisy thing to predict: a 1-star review can be about late shipping or the wrong size rather than the fabric. It builds a target from what buyers write about the material, and asks how far it agrees with the star average and whether listing fields can predict it.

| | Target | Input | Models | Metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Task A: material | Dominant fiber in the composition string | Title, bullets, and description, composition clauses removed | TF-IDF + logistic regression, TF-IDF + linear SVM, DistilBERT | Macro-F1 |
| Task B: rating | Average rating, 1 to 5 (products with at least 5 ratings) | Listing fields plus the material label | Mean baseline, random forest, XGBoost | RMSE and MAE |
| Task C: material sentiment | Share of a product's material sentences that are positive | Listing fields (the target is built from review text) | Mean baseline, XGBoost | RMSE and MAE |

The dataset poses three challenges. First, only 36.4% of listings in the ~250,000-product sample (90,979 of 249,762; 46% in the 10,000-record pilot) carry a composition string, so most products have no label and the labeled ones may not be representative. Second, the labels are heavily imbalanced: cotton and polyester make up 64.3% of labeled listings (33.0% and 31.3%), while metal has 152 labeled listings and rubber 300. Third, the label can mean different things across products, because a shoe's composition string often describes the sole rather than the upper.

## Proposed method in brief

For Task A we represent each cleaned listing as a TF-IDF vector of words and word pairs and classify it with a class-weighted linear model. On the ~250,000-product sample (10 classes, 18,318 test listings) the linear SVM reached a macro-F1 of 0.6203 and an accuracy of 0.7872, against 0.5799 and 0.7326 for logistic regression, so the SVM is higher on this test set on both metrics and on every class (confidence intervals are pending); we keep logistic regression in the demo because its class probabilities let the app show its confidence. Fine-tuned DistilBERT has no result on the larger sample yet. For Task B, XGBoost on listing fields with log-count sample weights is the only model that beat a weighted mean baseline on both error metrics (RMSE 0.4761 against 0.4955 on products with at least 5 ratings; confidence intervals pending), by a small margin that is itself our main finding about ratings; the random forest was worse than the baseline. For Task C we score each material-related review sentence with a transparent lexicon and average per product: across 209,830 such sentences, 15,397 products receive a sentiment score whose rank correlation with the star average is only 0.292, and listing fields did not beat the mean baseline on RMSE (0.7% worse; MAE 0.5% better), a difference we cannot call real until the confidence intervals are in.
