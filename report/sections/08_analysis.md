# Analysis

TF-IDF with a linear classifier works on Task A because of how sellers write. Listings are short and formulaic: they name the garment, the fit, the care instructions, and often the fabric, in a small shared vocabulary. Material is signaled by a few specific words ("leather", "denim", "fleece") rather than by sentence structure, so a bag-of-words representation loses little, and inverse document frequency gives exactly those rare, informative words the most weight. In a space of 60,000 sparse features, the classes are close to linearly separable wherever such words appear, which is consistent with the F1 of 0.943 (logistic regression) and 0.953 (SVM) that leather reaches on the 250k test set without any feature engineering. The same example warns about the label: in the pilot the top leather terms of the logistic model were footwear words such as "sole" and "rubber sole", so part of what the model learns is "this is a shoe", the label ambiguity noted in the introduction (we have not repeated that check on the 250k model).

The same property sets the limit. When the cleaned text contains the word of the listing's own true class, logistic regression is wrong 13.3% of the time on the 250k test set; when it does not, the error rises to 32.4% (our arithmetic, a word-boundary match over the eight named fiber classes). The likely reason is that the model then has to infer material from garment type, and polyester and cotton are sold as the same kinds of tees and dresses, which fits the confusion matrix, where polyester is sent to rayon, nylon, and cotton 574, 555, and 549 times; we did not test the garment-type explanation directly. The cleaning bug we found during the pilot shows the importance of such words from the other side: when the title and the bullets before each composition clause were accidentally erased, pilot macro-F1 fell from 0.557 to 0.360 (a pilot-only ablation, not rerun on the 250k sample), so a large share of the signal sat in a few words outside the composition string. The remaining errors are therefore partly a property of the listings, not of the model. A contextual model such as DistilBERT may still help on listings that describe a fabric indirectly ("breathable", "moisture-wicking", "soft knit"), but it cannot recover a material that the text never hints at. Published comparisons do not settle whether it will help. Wahba et al. (2022) found no significant gain from fine-tuned pretrained models, DistilBERT among them, over a linear SVM on TF-IDF features, while Galke and Scherp (2022), who found a wide MLP on bag-of-words input stronger than recent graph-based models, still found that fine-tuned BERT and DistilBERT outperform all the state-of-the-art models they compared. Our pilot leans toward the first result (pilot macro-F1 0.557 for logistic regression against 0.356 for DistilBERT, on the 903 pilot test listings), but the gap came mostly from acrylic, rayon, and the three rarest fibers, which DistilBERT rarely or never predicted, and DistilBERT was trained without the class weights the linear models use. Since NLP models "tend to perform poorly on less frequent classes" and the choice of loss function is one of the main remedies (Henning et al., 2023), a class-weighted DistilBERT on the larger sample is the fairer test of which result holds here; that 250k run is still pending, so we draw no conclusion about DistilBERT from the pilot. The two linear models raise a smaller version of the same question. On the 250k test set the SVM is higher than logistic regression on macro-F1 (0.6203 against 0.5799), accuracy (0.7872 against 0.7326), and every class, so the pilot's near tie did not persist on the larger sample. Wang and Manning (2012) showed that the ranking between linear baselines (Naive Bayes and SVMs in their study) changes with the task and with text length, so we read the SVM's lead as a result for this task and test set, with confidence intervals still pending, rather than as a general ranking.

On Task B the tree ensembles behave as their design predicts on a noisy target. A product's average rating is a mean of $n_i$ ratings with variance $\sigma^2/n_i$, and 34.6% of products have five ratings or fewer (rating standard deviation 1.10, against 0.34 for products with at least 50 ratings), so much of the target is noise. The results are consistent with how the two ensembles might handle noise, though we have not tested the mechanism. The random forest grows every tree to full depth, so each tree can end in leaves holding a single product and its noisy average; averaging 300 such trees reduces but may not remove that variance, and on the 250k test set the forest ends up worse than the weighted mean baseline (RMSE 0.7647 against 0.7512). XGBoost limits each tree to depth 6 and scales its contribution by 0.06, so no single tree can chase an outlier far, and the ensemble moves toward a prediction only where many products agree. The log-count weights help both by trusting averages built on many ratings more. A depth-limited forest would test this explanation directly, and we plan to run it on the larger sample.

Three properties of this dataset made the Task A solution work, and they define which datasets are similar. The label is embedded in the text by a deterministic pattern, so it can be extracted at scale without manual labeling, as long as the pattern is removed from the input. The input is short, seller-written text with a domain-specific vocabulary. And the classes are signaled by specific words rather than by tone or argument. Other e-commerce attributes share these properties, such as a product's category, the sleeve length or neckline of a garment, or the gemstone of a jewelry item, and so do medical or legal records where a coded field also appears in the free text. The same pipeline (extract the label, strip its source, TF-IDF, class-weighted linear model) should transfer to them directly. Product category is already a benchmark for short-title classification: in the SIGIR 2018 eCom Rakuten Data Challenge, 26 teams predicted the full taxonomy path of 0.2 million test product titles after training on 0.8 million, and the best of 28 systems reached a weighted F1 of 0.8513 (Lin et al., 2018). That task uses given labels rather than labels parsed from the text, and weighted F1 lets frequent categories count more than our macro-F1 does, so its numbers are not comparable with ours.

It would not transfer to tasks where meaning depends on context or long text, such as sentiment in full reviews or sarcasm, where contextual models are clearly better, nor to tasks whose answer lies outside the text, such as image-only attributes like color or pattern. Task B gives the most general lesson: average ratings in marketplace data are dominated by factors a listing cannot show and by noise from small rating counts, so any rating model on similar data should be compared against a weighted mean baseline before its gains are believed, and should be built from review or behavioral data rather than from listing metadata.
