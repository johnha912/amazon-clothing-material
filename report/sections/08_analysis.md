# Analysis

TF-IDF with a linear classifier works on Task A because listings are short and formulaic, and material is signaled by a few specific words ("leather", "denim", "fleece") rather than by sentence structure, so a bag-of-words representation loses little; leather's F1 is 0.943 (logistic regression) and 0.953 (SVM), though in the pilot its top terms were footwear words such as "sole", so the model partly learns "this is a shoe". The limit is the mirror image: of the 17,603 test listings in the eight named fiber classes, the 30.8% whose cleaned text contains the word of their own true class are misclassified by logistic regression 13.3% of the time, against 32.4% of the rest (our arithmetic from the saved predictions, a word-boundary match). A likely reason is that the model must then infer material from garment type, since polyester and cotton are sold as the same kinds of tees and dresses, which fits the confusion matrix in Appendix B (untested directly).

Published comparisons disagreed on whether DistilBERT would help: Wahba et al. (2022) found no significant gain from fine-tuned pretrained models over a linear SVM on TF-IDF features, while Galke and Scherp (2022) found fine-tuned BERT and DistilBERT ahead of the models they compared. Here DistilBERT is ahead of the SVM by 0.0555 macro-F1; those studies used other datasets and setups, and ours is one training seed per setting, so we do not read it as confirming either. The class-weighted DistilBERT, the fairer test suggested by the loss literature (Henning et al., 2023), scored lower than the plain one; it predicts most mid-sized and small classes more often than they occur (nylon 1,451 times for 1,110 true listings), as the class-weighted logistic regression does, one untested reason. The SVM's lead over logistic regression is a result for this task and test set only, since Wang and Manning (2012) showed that rankings between linear baselines change with the task and with text length.

DistilBERT's remaining errors look like the linear models': of its 3,526 errors, 2,395 are also SVM errors, about two thirds (our arithmetic), and 2,898 of its 3,215 errors on the eight single-fiber classes (90%) are listings that do not name their own fiber (`python analysis/material_bert.py --error-analysis`, seed 42; Appendix B.1). The three real errors in Appendix B show the pattern: the dominant fiber is unnamed in the cleaned text or has its percentages blanked by our leakage rule. We did not measure how much evidence any text model could recover, so this sets no ceiling.

On Task B the random forest grows every tree to full depth, so each tree can end in leaves holding a single product and its noisy average, and averaging 300 such trees may not remove that variance; the forest ends up worse than the weighted mean baseline, while XGBoost limits each tree to depth 6 and scales its contribution by 0.06, so no single tree can chase an outlier far. Task C shows what the star average is made of: its rank correlation with material sentiment is only 0.292, and about 81% of 1-star reviews contain no material sentence the lexicon matches, so we think many low averages reflect things other than fabric (we did not classify what those reviews discuss). Listing features improve the star average by 4.4% in RMSE but did not beat the mean baseline on material sentiment, so a listing may carry signals about the overall experience but little about how the fabric feels (untested); the part of a rating a listing can predict is thus not clearly the part about the material.

Task A worked because the label sits in the text as a deterministic pattern, the input is short seller-written text, and the classes are signaled by specific words rather than by tone. The same pipeline should transfer to other e-commerce attributes, such as sleeve length or a gemstone, but not to tasks where meaning depends on context or long text; and a rating model should be compared with a weighted mean baseline before its gains are believed.
