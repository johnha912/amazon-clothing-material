# Analysis

TF-IDF with a linear classifier works on Task A because of how sellers write. Listings are short and formulaic: they name the garment, the fit, the care instructions, and often the fabric, in a small shared vocabulary. Material is signaled by a few specific words ("leather", "denim", "fleece") rather than by sentence structure, so a bag-of-words representation loses little, and inverse document frequency gives exactly those rare, informative words the most weight. In a space of 60,000 sparse features, the classes are close to linearly separable wherever such words appear, which is why a linear model reaches 0.94 F1 on leather without any feature engineering. The same example warns about the label: the top leather terms are footwear words such as "sole" and "rubber sole", so part of what the model learns is "this is a shoe", the label ambiguity noted in the introduction.

The same property sets the limit. When the text names a fiber outside the removed clauses, the model is wrong 22.0% of the time; when it does not, the error rises to 37.8%, because the model then has to infer material from garment type, and polyester and cotton are sold as the same kinds of tees and dresses. The cleaning bug we found shows this from the other side: when the title and the bullets before each composition clause were accidentally erased, macro-F1 fell from 0.557 to 0.360, so a large share of the signal sits in a few words outside the composition string. The remaining errors are therefore partly a property of the listings, not of the model. A contextual model such as DistilBERT may still help on listings that describe a fabric indirectly ("breathable", "moisture-wicking", "soft knit"), but it cannot recover a material that the text never hints at.

On Task B the tree ensembles behave as their design predicts on a noisy target. A product's average rating is a mean of $n_i$ ratings with variance $\sigma^2/n_i$, and 15.8% of products have five ratings or fewer, so much of the target is noise. The results are consistent with how the two ensembles handle noise. The random forest grows every tree to full depth, so each tree can end in leaves holding a single product and its noisy average; averaging 300 such trees reduces but does not remove that variance, and the forest ends up tied with the baseline. XGBoost limits each tree to depth 6 and scales its contribution by 0.06, so no single tree can chase an outlier far, and the ensemble moves toward a prediction only where many products agree. The log-count weights help both by trusting averages built on many ratings more. A depth-limited forest would test this explanation directly, and we plan to run it on the larger sample.

Three properties of this dataset made the Task A solution work, and they define which datasets are similar. The label is embedded in the text by a deterministic pattern, so it can be extracted at scale without manual labeling, as long as the pattern is removed from the input. The input is short, seller-written text with a domain-specific vocabulary. And the classes are signaled by specific words rather than by tone or argument. Other e-commerce attributes share these properties, such as a product's category, the sleeve length or neckline of a garment, or the gemstone of a jewelry item, and so do medical or legal records where a coded field also appears in the free text. The same pipeline (extract the label, strip its source, TF-IDF, class-weighted linear model) should transfer to them directly.

It would not transfer to tasks where meaning depends on context or long text, such as sentiment in full reviews or sarcasm, where contextual models are clearly better, nor to tasks whose answer lies outside the text, such as image-only attributes like color or pattern. Task B gives the most general lesson: average ratings in marketplace data are dominated by factors a listing cannot show and by noise from small rating counts, so any rating model on similar data should be compared against a weighted mean baseline before its gains are believed, and should be built from review or behavioral data rather than from listing metadata.
