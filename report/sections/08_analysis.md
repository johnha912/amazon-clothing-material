# Analysis

TF-IDF with a linear classifier works on Task A because of how sellers write: listings are short and formulaic, in a small shared vocabulary, and material is signaled by a few specific words ("leather", "denim", "fleece") rather than by sentence structure, so a bag-of-words representation loses little and inverse document frequency gives those rare, informative words the most weight. Wherever such words appear the classes are close to linearly separable, consistent with leather's F1 of 0.943 (logistic regression) and 0.953 (SVM). The same example warns about the label: in the pilot, the top leather terms were footwear words such as "sole" and "rubber sole", so part of what the model learns is "this is a shoe", the label ambiguity noted in the introduction (we have not repeated that check on the 250k model). The limit is the mirror image: when the cleaned text contains the word of the listing's own true class, logistic regression is wrong 13.3% of the time; when it does not, the error rises to 32.4% (our arithmetic, a word-boundary match over the eight named fiber classes). The likely reason is that the model must then infer material from garment type — polyester and cotton are sold as the same kinds of tees and dresses — which fits the confusion matrix, where polyester is sent to rayon, nylon, and cotton 574, 555, and 549 times; we did not test the garment-type explanation directly.

Published comparisons disagreed on whether DistilBERT would help here: Wahba et al. (2022) found no significant gain from fine-tuned pretrained models, DistilBERT among them, over a linear SVM on TF-IDF features, while Galke and Scherp (2022) found fine-tuned BERT and DistilBERT ahead of the models they compared. On our test listings DistilBERT is ahead of the SVM by 0.0555 macro-F1 (paired interval 0.0325 to 0.0763), a different outcome from the pilot, where it ranked below the linear models (0.356 against 0.557 for the best of them on 903 pilot test listings). Those studies used other datasets and setups, so we do not read our result as confirming either, and it comes from one training seed per setting. The class-weighted DistilBERT, which the loss literature suggested as the fairer test (NLP models "tend to perform poorly on less frequent classes", Henning et al., 2023), scored lower than the plain one (0.6576 against 0.6758; paired interval −0.0343 to −0.0018); the weighted model over-predicts most mid-sized and small classes, as the class-weighted logistic regression does (nylon predicted 1,451 times for 1,110 true listings, rayon 1,343 for 975, wool 266 for 169; metal, 25 for 27, is the exception), one plausible reason for the lower score that we did not test. The linear models raise a smaller version of the same question: the SVM is higher than logistic regression on macro-F1, accuracy, and every class, so the pilot's near tie did not persist, but Wang and Manning (2012) showed that rankings between linear baselines change with the task and with text length, so we read the SVM's lead as a result for this task and test set — its paired bootstrap interval (+0.029 to +0.052 of macro-F1) excludes zero — rather than as a general ranking.

DistilBERT's remaining errors look like the linear models' (`results/material_bert_error_summary.json`, `python analysis/material_bert.py --error-analysis`, seed 42). Of its 3,526 errors, 2,395 are also SVM errors, about two thirds (our arithmetic); the two models agree on 15,041 listings, DistilBERT alone is right on 1,503 and the SVM alone on 1,131. The largest cells are cotton predicted as polyester (641) and polyester as cotton (580). On the 17,603 test listings of the eight single-fiber classes, 30.8% contain the word of their own class, and DistilBERT errs on 5.8% of those against the SVM's 8.8%, but on 23.8% of the rest against 25.7%, so 2,898 of its 3,215 errors on these rows (90%) are listings that do not name their own fiber; context may help most where a fiber is named in passing or several are named, which we did not test. Three real errors show why. A Van Heusen cotton shirt, predicted polyester, names no material anywhere in its cleaned text ("Wrinkle-resistant and stain-repellant" is the kind of cue a model can read as synthetic); an AONIJIE nylon hydration vest, predicted polyester, names none of the 26 material names in `src/labels.py`; and a Modfine shirt labeled polyester, predicted cotton, reads "Cotton,Polyester … Material: , ." with its percentages blanked by our leakage rule, so its dominant fiber cannot be read from the text. We did not measure how much evidence any text model could still recover, so this describes the errors and does not set a ceiling.

On Task B the tree ensembles behave as their design predicts on a noisy target. A product's average rating is a mean of $n_i$ ratings with variance $\sigma^2/n_i$, and before filtering 34.6% of products have five ratings or fewer (rating standard deviation 1.10, against 0.34 for products with at least 50 ratings), so much of the target is noise; Task B therefore keeps only products with at least 5 ratings, which removes 30.7% of the products that have an average, and the kept averages stay noisy (the 200 largest misses have a median of 8 ratings). The random forest grows every tree to full depth, so each tree can end in leaves holding a single product and its noisy average, and averaging 300 such trees reduces but may not remove that variance; the forest ends up worse than the weighted mean baseline (RMSE 0.5044 against 0.4955, on the filtered set). XGBoost limits each tree to depth 6 and scales its contribution by 0.06, so no single tree can chase an outlier far, and the ensemble moves toward a prediction only where many products agree.

Task C shows what the star average is made of: its rank correlation with material sentiment is only 0.292, and about 81% of 1-star reviews contain no material sentence the lexicon matches (`results/review_sentiment_run.json`, key `material_mention_share_by_star`), so we think many low averages reflect things other than fabric, though we did not classify what those reviews are about. On the same products, listing features improve the star average by 4.4% in RMSE but did not beat the mean baseline on material sentiment (RMSE 0.7% worse; MAE 0.5% better); the paired intervals for XGBoost minus baseline include zero (RMSE −0.0008 to +0.0045, MAE −0.0036 to +0.0014; `results/ci_summary.csv`), so the two do not differ measurably. A listing may carry signals about the overall experience a buyer will report (price band, department, and the expectations they set) while carrying little about how the fabric itself feels, which we think is only known after the garment is worn; we did not test this. Read together, Tasks B and C suggest that the part of a rating a listing can predict is not the part about the material, and that a star average is likely a weak verdict on fabric quality.

Three properties made the Task A solution work, and they define which datasets are similar: the label is embedded in the text by a deterministic pattern, extractable at scale without manual labeling once the pattern is removed from the input; the input is short, seller-written text with a domain-specific vocabulary; and the classes are signaled by specific words rather than by tone or argument. The same pipeline (extract the label, strip its source, TF-IDF, class-weighted linear model) should transfer wherever these hold: other e-commerce attributes, such as a garment's sleeve length or neckline or a jewelry item's gemstone, and medical or legal records where a coded field also appears in the free text. It would not transfer to tasks where meaning depends on context or long text, such as sentiment in full reviews or sarcasm, nor to tasks whose answer lies outside the text, such as image-only attributes like color or pattern. Tasks B and C give the most general lesson: average ratings in marketplace data are likely dominated by factors a listing cannot show and by noise from small rating counts, so any rating model on similar data should be compared against a weighted mean baseline before its gains are believed, and a claim about one product attribute should be measured in the review text about that attribute, not read off the star average.
