# Conclusion

A product's dominant material can be recovered from its Amazon listing with useful accuracy, but it says little about how buyers will rate the product, and the rating itself says little about the material. On the ~250,000-product sample, TF-IDF with a class-weighted linear SVM reached a macro-F1 of 0.6203 and an accuracy of 0.7872 across ten materials (class-weighted logistic regression: 0.5799 and 0.7326), and fine-tuned DistilBERT did better still at 0.6758 (0.6512–0.6968) and 0.8075, 0.0555 above the SVM on the same test listings (paired interval 0.0325 to 0.0763, which excludes zero; `python analysis/material_bert.py --overwrite`, seed 42), with leather nearly solved and the main errors among polyester, nylon, rayon, and cotton (95% CIs: logistic regression 0.5648–0.5957, SVM 0.6027–0.6359; the paired difference interval, +0.029 to +0.052, excludes zero). For ratings, the best model, XGBoost, improved a weighted mean baseline by only 3.9% in RMSE (0.4761 against 0.4955, our arithmetic) on products with at least 5 ratings, and the random forest was worse than the baseline, because average ratings are compressed, noisy for products with few ratings, and driven by factors that listings do not show. Task C completes the picture from the buyers' side: what reviewers write about the fabric correlates only weakly with the star average (Spearman 0.292), about four in five 1-star reviews contain no material sentence the lexicon matches, and listing fields did not beat the mean baseline on RMSE (0.7% worse; MAE 0.5% better), where the paired RMSE interval, −0.0008 to +0.0045, includes zero, so a star average should likely not be read as a verdict on material quality.

For anyone who wants to use this work, we weigh accuracy against cost. DistilBERT is the most accurate material model we tested (macro-F1 0.6758 against 0.6203 for the SVM, a gap larger than test-set noise), but it needs a GPU (1,719.8 s for the two settings we trained, on one RTX 5090 Laptop GPU) and a trained copy of the pretrained network, while the TF-IDF models train in minutes on a CPU and are what the demo runs. Where the extra 0.0555 of macro-F1 is worth that cost, use DistilBERT; otherwise a TF-IDF linear model is a reasonable default. Between the two linear models the SVM is higher on this test set on both macro-F1 and accuracy; logistic regression remains attractive for an application because its word weights can be inspected and its probabilities let it show only confident predictions, and an SVM would need calibration to do the same. The demo currently loads logistic regression. [TODO: team decision on which model the demo ships.] We do not recommend predicting ratings from listing metadata; a product team that needs rating forecasts should start from review text or return data, and a team that needs to know how buyers judge the fabric should measure the review sentences about the fabric, as Task C does, rather than reuse the star average.

The next steps are cross-validated tuning of the linear models, and for DistilBERT more than one training seed and an epoch search that goes past 3, since validation macro-F1 was still rising at epoch 3 (whether more epochs help is untested). A company running this at high scale should keep the linear model as the default, since it needs no GPU and processes millions of listings cheaply, route only low-confidence listings to DistilBERT or human review, retrain whenever sellers' writing changes, and present predicted materials as suggestions rather than seller-provided facts.
