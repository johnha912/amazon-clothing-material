# Conclusion

A product's dominant material can be recovered from its Amazon listing with useful accuracy, but it says little about how buyers will rate the product. On the pilot, TF-IDF with class-weighted logistic regression reached a macro-F1 of 0.557 and an accuracy of 0.677 across nine materials (a linear SVM: 0.524 and 0.736), with leather nearly solved and the main errors between polyester and cotton. For ratings, the best model, XGBoost, improved a weighted mean baseline by only 1.4% in RMSE, because average ratings are compressed, noisy for products with few ratings, and driven by factors that listings do not show.

For anyone who wants to use this work, we recommend a TF-IDF linear model for material. Logistic regression and the linear SVM are effectively tied on the pilot; we would deploy logistic regression because it trains in seconds, its word weights can be inspected, and its probabilities let an application show only confident predictions. We do not recommend predicting ratings from listing metadata; a product team that needs rating forecasts should start from review text or return data instead.

More data would help most where the pilot is thin: the 250,000-product sample will give rare fibers enough examples, and cross-validated tuning and DistilBERT can then be compared fairly. A company running this at high scale should keep the linear model as the default, since it needs no GPU and processes millions of listings cheaply, route only low-confidence listings to DistilBERT or human review, and retrain whenever sellers' writing changes. It should also present predicted materials as suggestions rather than seller-provided facts.
