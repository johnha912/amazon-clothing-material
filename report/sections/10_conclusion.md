# Conclusion

A product's dominant material can be recovered from its Amazon listing with useful accuracy, but it says little about how buyers will rate the product, and the rating itself says little about the material. On the ~250,000-product sample, fine-tuned DistilBERT reached the highest macro-F1 across ten materials, 0.6758, 0.0555 above the TF-IDF linear SVM at 0.6203 (paired interval 0.0325 to 0.0763). For ratings, XGBoost improved a weighted mean baseline by only 3.9% in RMSE (0.4761 against 0.4955, our arithmetic), probably because average ratings are compressed, noisy, and driven by factors that listings do not show. What reviewers write about the fabric correlates only weakly with the star average (Spearman 0.292), and listing fields did not beat the mean baseline on material sentiment (paired RMSE interval −0.0008 to +0.0045, which includes zero), so a star average should likely not be read as a verdict on material quality.

DistilBERT is the most accurate material model we tested but needs a GPU (1,719.8 s for the two settings), while the TF-IDF models train in minutes on a CPU; where the extra 0.0555 of macro-F1 is worth that cost, use DistilBERT, and otherwise a linear model is a reasonable default. The demo ships the SVM with sigmoid calibration fitted on five training folds (seed 42), so it can show class probabilities; this calibrated model scores macro-F1 0.643 on the test set and agrees with the plain SVM on 92.5% of test listings (`python analysis/material_tfidf.py`, `results/demo_model.csv`; no interval computed). We do not recommend predicting ratings from listing metadata. Next steps are cross-validated tuning of the linear models, and for DistilBERT more seeds and an epoch search past 3.
