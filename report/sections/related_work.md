# Related Work

Our project sits where four lines of research meet: large public datasets of Amazon products and reviews, the extraction of product attributes from listing text, the choice between sparse linear text models and pretrained transformers, and aspect-based sentiment analysis of reviews. Rating prediction on Amazon data frames our second task, and work on why star ratings are noisy frames our third. Each paper below was chosen because its findings can be set against ours.

Our data come from Amazon Reviews 2023, released by Hou et al. (2024) together with BLaIR, a series of pretrained sentence embedding models that learn correlations between item metadata and natural language context for retrieval and recommendation. The dataset contains over 570 million reviews and 48 million items from 33 categories, which the authors describe as a significant expansion over previous versions. Hou et al. use item metadata as text to be embedded and matched against queries, without supervised labels on the items themselves. We use the Clothing, Shoes and Jewelry category for supervised tasks: classifying a product's dominant material from its listing, regressing its average rating, and scoring what buyers say about its material in review text and predicting that score from the same listing fields, with labels and inputs separated by explicit leakage rules.

Product attribute extraction treats a listing's attributes as values to be found in its text. Zheng et al. (2018) proposed OpenTag, which frames extraction as sequence tagging over product titles, descriptions, and bullets with a bidirectional LSTM and a conditional random field, adds attention for interpretability, and uses active learning to reduce annotation. On Amazon products in three domains (dog food, detergents, and cameras), it discovered new attribute values from as few as 150 annotated samples with an F-score of 83%. Yang et al. (2022) scaled the task up with MAVE, a dataset of 2.2 million Amazon products with 3 million attribute-value annotations across 1,257 categories. Material is one of the five frequent attributes the authors evaluate individually, and on it the BERT-based AVEQA model reaches an F1 of 98.95% and an attribute-dependent OpenTag 90.69%. Both papers extract a value that appears in the text. Our task asks a harder question. Because we parse the label from the composition string (for example "60% cotton, 40% polyester") and then remove that string from the input, the model must infer the material from indirect evidence such as the product type and style words. Zou et al. (2024) call this setting implicit attribute value extraction: their ImplicitAVE benchmark, built from MAVE with product images collected from Amazon, targets values that are never stated in the text, and they report that Clothing is the most challenging of its five domains. Removing the composition string is also a guard against leakage in the sense of Kaufman, Rosset, and Perlich (2011), the introduction of information about the target that should not be legitimately available to mine from; when a first cleaning pass missed composition strings written in other forms, our scores rose by several points, which is the inflation their framework predicts. Our scores are therefore not comparable with those of extraction systems, since they measure inference rather than lookup.

Our material classifiers compare a sparse baseline with a pretrained transformer. Sanh et al. (2019) introduced DistilBERT, which applies knowledge distillation during pretraining with a triple loss combining language modeling, distillation, and cosine-distance terms, and reported a model 40% smaller and 60% faster than BERT that retains 97% of its language understanding capabilities. Its size made fine-tuning on a single Colab GPU practical for us. Wahba et al. (2022) compared four pretrained models (BERT, DistilBERT, RoBERTa, and XLM), off the shelf and fine-tuned, with a linear SVM on TF-IDF features, on three public datasets and a real-world dataset full of domain-specific vocabulary, and found that the pretrained models gave no significant gain over the linear SVM. Short, jargon-heavy product listings resemble their real-world case, so we treat a well-tuned TF-IDF model as a serious competitor rather than a straw man, and we compare DistilBERT with it on the same test split.

An earlier generation of Amazon review data, from the same senior author, was introduced together with a rating prediction model. McAuley and Leskovec (2013) collected about 35 million Amazon reviews split into 26 top-level categories and proposed Hidden Factors as Topics (HFT), which ties the latent factors of a recommender system to topics learned from review text. HFT achieved the lowest mean squared error on 30 of the 33 datasets they studied, helped most where users or products had few ratings, and on subjective categories such as clothing and shoes improved on state-of-the-art methods by up to 20%. Their target is one user's rating of one product, and their inputs include the user's identity and the review text. Our Task B predicts a product's average rating from its listing alone and, by design, uses no review text, reviewer identity, or rating history, which may explain why it improves only modestly on a mean baseline. The star average may also be a noisy target in the first place. Hu, Zhang, and Pavlou (2009) show that online ratings follow a J-shaped distribution produced by purchasing bias and under-reporting bias, so averages crowd the top of the scale and say little about true quality. A low average can therefore reflect who chose to review and why, not only the product's material.

That noise motivates Task C, which builds its target from the review text itself and predicts it from the same listing fields as Task B. Hu and Liu (2004) defined feature-based summarization of customer reviews: mine the product features that customers comment on, identify the opinion sentences about each feature, and decide whether each is positive or negative, using opinion words grown from a seed list of positive and negative adjectives through WordNet. Material is one such product feature of a clothing item. Task C adapts Hu and Liu's design to a single, fixed feature: it keeps only review sentences that name a fabric or describe how it feels, scores them with a transparent lexicon of material-specific positive and negative terms, and checks that lexicon against a pretrained sentiment model. We have no manual annotations of material opinions, so the lexicon and its printed false-positive examples, rather than a gold standard, are how we audit the target.

Taken together, these papers place our project between established settings. Attribute extraction succeeds when the value is written in the text, rating prediction succeeds when reviews and user histories are available, and aspect-based sentiment analysis reads opinions about named product features. We study the gaps between them: what a listing implies about its material when the composition is hidden, what it implies about its rating before any review exists, and whether what buyers say about the material tells a cleaner story than the stars they give.
