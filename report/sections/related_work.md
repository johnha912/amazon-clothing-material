# Related Work

Our project sits where three lines of research meet: large public datasets of Amazon products and reviews, the extraction of product attributes from listing text, and the choice between sparse linear text models and pretrained transformers. A fourth line, rating prediction on Amazon data, frames our second task. We discuss six papers, each chosen because its findings can be set against ours.

Our data come from Amazon Reviews 2023, released by Hou et al. (2024) together with BLaIR, a series of pretrained sentence embedding models that learn correlations between item metadata and natural language context for retrieval and recommendation. The dataset contains over 570 million reviews and 48 million items from 33 categories, which the authors describe as a significant expansion over previous versions, and the paper evaluates BLaIR on a new complex product search task with a semi-synthetic evaluation set built with a large language model. Hou et al. use item metadata as text to be embedded and matched against queries, without supervised labels on the items themselves. We use the metadata of one category, Clothing, Shoes and Jewelry, for two supervised tasks: classifying a product's dominant material and regressing its average rating, with labels and inputs separated by explicit leakage rules.

An earlier generation of Amazon review data, from the same senior author, was introduced together with a rating prediction model. McAuley and Leskovec (2013) collected about 35 million Amazon reviews split into 26 top-level categories and proposed Hidden Factors as Topics (HFT), which ties the latent factors of a recommender system to topics learned from review text. HFT achieved the lowest mean squared error on 30 of the 33 datasets they studied, helped most where users or products had few ratings, and on subjective categories such as clothing and shoes improved on state-of-the-art methods by up to 20%. Their target is one user's rating of one product, and their inputs include the user's identity and the review text. Our Task B predicts a product's average rating from its listing alone and, by design, uses no review text, reviewer identity, or rating history. Our XGBoost model improves on the mean baseline by only 1.4% in RMSE (0.5780 against 0.5863 on our 10,000-record pilot). This small gain may reflect that the signals HFT relies on are the ones our leakage rules exclude, although we have not tested that explanation.

Product attribute extraction treats a listing's attributes as values to be found in its text. Zheng et al. (2018) proposed OpenTag, which frames extraction as sequence tagging over product titles, descriptions, and bullets with a bidirectional LSTM and a conditional random field, adds attention for interpretability, and uses active learning to reduce annotation. On Amazon products in three domains (dog food, detergents, and cameras), it discovered new attribute values from as few as 150 annotated samples with an F-score of 83%. Yang et al. (2022) scaled the task up with MAVE, a dataset of 2.2 million Amazon products with 3 million attribute-value annotations across 1,257 categories. Material is one of the five frequent attributes the authors evaluate individually, and on it the BERT-based AVEQA model reaches an F1 of 98.95% and an attribute-dependent OpenTag 90.69%. Both papers extract a value that appears in the text, and MAVE labels an example as having no value when no span can be found. Our task asks the opposite question. Because we parse the label from the composition string (for example "60% cotton, 40% polyester") and then remove that string from the input, the model must infer the material from indirect evidence in the title, features, and description, such as the product type and style words. Our scores are therefore not comparable with theirs, since they measure inference rather than extraction, and listings without a stated composition are exactly the cases where an extractor would return nothing.

Our material classifiers compare a sparse baseline with a pretrained transformer. Sanh et al. (2019) introduced DistilBERT, which applies knowledge distillation during pretraining with a triple loss combining language modeling, distillation, and cosine-distance terms, and reported a model 40% smaller and 60% faster than BERT that retains 97% of its language understanding capabilities. Its size made fine-tuning on a single Colab GPU practical for us. Wahba et al. (2022) compared four fine-tuned pretrained models (BERT, DistilBERT, RoBERTa, and XLM) with a linear SVM on TF-IDF features over unigrams to trigrams, on three public datasets and a private, imbalanced dataset of IT support tickets full of domain jargon, and found that the pretrained models, even fine-tuned, gave no significant gain over the linear SVM. Our pilot agrees in direction only: TF-IDF with logistic regression reaches a macro-F1 of 0.557 and DistilBERT 0.356, from a single seed on 903 test listings. DistilBERT matches TF-IDF with logistic regression on accuracy (0.690 against 0.677) and does well on the most frequent fibers, but it predicts almost none of the rarest classes. One possible explanation, which we have not tested, is that it was fine-tuned with an unweighted loss on 3,641 training listings while the TF-IDF models use balanced class weights; multi-fiber listings and truncated inputs may also contribute. We therefore do not read the pilot as a general verdict on transformers, and treat the larger sample as the fair test.

Taken together, these papers place our project between two established settings. Attribute extraction succeeds when the value is written in the text, and rating prediction succeeds when reviews and user histories are available. We study the gap between them: what a listing implies about its material when the composition is hidden, and what it implies about its rating before any review exists.
