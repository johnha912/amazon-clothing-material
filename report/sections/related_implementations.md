# Related Implementations

Public Kaggle notebooks on Amazon Reviews 2023 mostly predict star ratings from review text, build recommenders, or describe the data. In our search of public notebooks on this dataset we did not find one that predicts a product's material or its average rating from the listing alone, so the three below are the closest neighbors, each compared on what it predicts, from which inputs, and how it is evaluated.

"Multi-Class Star Rating Prediction on Amazon Video Games Reviews using PySpark" by Keerthana Uppala (https://www.kaggle.com/code/keerthanauppala00/cn7030-multi-class-star-rating-prediction-on-amazo), a course project notebook copied from an earlier notebook by Wajahat Waheed, predicts the star rating of each individual review in a 200,000-review sample of the Video Games category. It uses TF-IDF features and a random forest on PySpark and reaches 63.71% accuracy and 49.61% F1. Its input differs from ours in a fundamental way: the model reads the very review whose rating it predicts, so the author's opinion is already in the input. Our Task B predicts a product's average rating from its listing (price, whether a price is listed, department, material, and title and text length) and, by our leakage rules, uses no feature derived from the product's own reviews or ratings. That setting answers a question a seller can act on before any review exists, which a review-level classifier cannot, and it is honest about how little signal the listing holds: our XGBoost model improves on a constant mean baseline by only 1.4% in RMSE (0.5780 vs. 0.5863).

"Recommendation system Optimised" by PandeyMritunjay (https://www.kaggle.com/code/pandeymritunjay/recommendation-system-optimised) works on the Amazon Fashion category of the same dataset, about 826,000 products and the closest neighbor to our Clothing, Shoes and Jewelry category. It builds each product's text from the title, features, and description, as we do, encodes it with a fine-tuned MPNet sentence-embedding model, and serves recommendations through a FAISS index. Because the goal is retrieval, it has no train/test split and no supervised metric, and it drops the price column because about 94% of prices are missing. Our project keeps price and models its absence with a `has_price` indicator, since in our sample prices are missing for 45.7% of listings and not at random across departments. Every model is scored on one seeded train/test split shared by both tasks, and results are broken down by class (per-class F1 and the confusion matrix for Task A, residuals by material for Task B), so model choices can be compared and their errors explained.

"Amazon Reviews 2023 – Analyzing Visual Keywords" by Suraj Neupane (https://www.kaggle.com/code/surajneupane/amazon-reviews-2023-analyzing-visual-keywords) is an exploratory analysis of the All Beauty reviews. It keeps reviews of three stars or fewer and finds that 29.07% of them mention visual keywords such as size, color, or look. It trains no model, so it describes what low-rated reviews talk about without testing whether a low rating can be predicted. Our work asks that second question for clothing, using only what is known before any review is written. Our ten largest Task B errors are all products rated 1.0 that the model, which sees no review text, scores between 4.24 and 4.55, so our listing-only model did not flag them.

Taken together, these notebooks show two shortcuts our project avoids: Uppala's notebook predicts a rating from text that already contains the reviewer's opinion, while PandeyMritunjay's and Neupane's use the data without a held-out evaluation. Our tasks are narrower, but every score comes from a held-out test split and the inputs exclude anything derived from the target.
